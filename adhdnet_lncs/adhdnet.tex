\begin{abstract}
Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder whose diagnosis primarily relies on behavioral and clinical assessment, motivating the investigation of neuroimaging-based computational approaches. However, many deep learning models for neuroimaging employ increasingly complex architectures with substantial parameter and computational requirements. This work presents \emph{ADHDNet}, a lightweight multimodal 3D convolutional neural network for ADHD classification using structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) and phenotypic information. The framework employs depthwise-separable 3D convolutional blocks to reduce model parameters while retaining volumetric feature extraction capability, a single three-dimensional efficient channel attention module to recalibrate the final encoder feature channels, and an adaptive gated fusion module to combine MRI-derived and phenotypic representations. Experiments were conducted on a multisite subset of the ADHD-200 dataset comprising 497 subjects. Structural MRI volumes were resized to $64\times64\times64$, normalized, and combined with five phenotypic features. ADHDNet contains 71,552 trainable parameters (0.0716M) and requires 0.19 GFLOPs per volume. On a stratified held-out validation set, it achieved 90.00\% accuracy and an AUC of 0.9543, and in stratified 5-fold cross-validation it achieved $87.52\pm4.61\%$ accuracy and an AUC of $0.951\pm0.017$. Compared with a conventional Conv3D multimodal model containing 1.376M parameters, ADHDNet uses 94.8\% fewer parameters and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs while reaching comparable cross-validated AUC ($0.957\pm0.020$). A component-wise ablation shows, however, that the phenotypic branch provides most of the discriminative information: the MRI-only variant performed close to chance. These results indicate that a compact multimodal 3D architecture can match a much larger model at a fraction of its cost, while highlighting the need to evaluate MRI-derived and phenotypic contributions separately.

\keywords{ADHD \and Structural MRI \and Multimodal learning \and 3D CNN \and Depthwise-separable convolution \and Efficient channel attention \and Lightweight deep learning}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. Diagnosis primarily relies on behavioral assessment and clinical evaluation, which can be challenging because ADHD symptoms may overlap with those of other developmental and psychiatric conditions. These challenges have motivated the investigation of neuroimaging-based computational approaches for identifying brain patterns associated with ADHD~\cite{zhangjames2023,tian2024review,lohani2025review}.

Among neuroimaging modalities, structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) provides non-invasive information about brain anatomy and has been widely investigated for computational ADHD analysis~\cite{chen2025meta}. Earlier machine learning studies typically relied on manually derived neuroanatomical measurements, such as gray-matter volume and cortical thickness, together with demographic or personal characteristics. For example, Lohani and Rana~\cite{lohani2023} combined structural MRI-derived features with personal characteristics and reported a maximum accuracy of 75\% using conventional machine learning classifiers on an age-balanced ADHD dataset.

The adoption of deep learning has subsequently enabled direct learning of imaging representations from neuroimaging data, with recent ADHD studies exploring convolutional networks, multimodal architectures, attention mechanisms, and ensemble approaches~\cite{firouzi2024,hu2024,cao2024,pedrollo2026}. While these approaches can improve representation learning, increasing architectural complexity may also increase the number of trainable parameters and computational requirements. Consequently, parameter efficiency is becoming an important consideration for neuroimaging models intended for resource-constrained environments. Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2025} explicitly investigated parameter-efficient ADHD classification using a lightweight attention-driven KAN framework and reported 79.25\% accuracy with only a few thousand parameters.

However, relatively limited work has investigated parameter-efficient multimodal architectures that directly process volumetric structural MRI while incorporating phenotypic information. In particular, there remains a need to examine whether volumetric structural information can be modeled using a compact 3D architecture without relying on large conventional convolutional backbones or complex multimodal fusion mechanisms. This study therefore investigates whether a compact 3D architecture can jointly model structural MRI and phenotypic information while substantially reducing parameter requirements.

To address this gap, we propose \emph{ADHDNet}, a lightweight adaptive multimodal 3D CNN designed to jointly exploit volumetric structural MRI and phenotypic information under a constrained parameter budget. The architecture combines depthwise-separable 3D convolution for efficient volumetric feature extraction, 3D efficient channel attention for feature recalibration, and adaptive gated fusion for multimodal integration. The main contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{lightweight 3D MRI encoder} based on depthwise-separable Conv3D for parameter-efficient volumetric representation learning.
  \item Integration of lightweight \textbf{3D efficient channel attention (ECA)} within the hierarchical MRI encoder for channel-wise feature recalibration.
  \item An \textbf{adaptive gated fusion} mechanism for sample-dependent integration of structural MRI and phenotypic representations.
  \item An \textbf{explicit evaluation of parameter and computational efficiency} against a conventional multimodal Conv3D model on a multisite ADHD-200 cohort, complemented by 5-fold cross-validation and a component-wise ablation study.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
%======================================================================

\subsection{MRI-Based ADHD Classification}
MRI-based machine learning has been widely investigated for ADHD classification~\cite{zhangjames2023,tian2024review,lohani2025review}. Earlier approaches generally relied on manually extracted neuroanatomical or functional connectivity features followed by conventional classifiers. Lohani and Rana~\cite{lohani2023} extracted gray-matter volume and cortical\-thickness features from structural MRI, combined them with personal characteristics, and achieved 75\% accuracy using conventional classifiers with repeated ten-fold cross\-validation. More recent studies have adopted deep learning to learn representations directly from neuroimaging data. CNN-based approaches have been investigated for fMRI-based ADHD classification~\cite{firouzi2024,gulhan2025}, graph-theoretical features of resting-state networks have been combined with ensemble classifiers~\cite{zamanzadeh2024}, graph convolutional networks with multi-head attention have been applied to hybrid-order brain networks~\cite{wu2025hagcn}, and Pedrollo et al.~\cite{pedrollo2026} reported CNN ensemble-based classification using resting-state fMRI in a cohort of 623 participants. These studies demonstrate the potential of deep neural networks for learning discriminative neuroimaging representations; however, many focus primarily on predictive performance achieved through increasingly complex architectures.

\subsection{Multimodal ADHD Analysis}
Phenotypic variables can provide information that is complementary to neuroimaging measurements. Age, sex, medication status, and clinical characteristics may capture information that is not directly represented in image features, and Lohani and Rana~\cite{lohani2023} provide evidence for the usefulness of combining structural MRI-derived characteristics with personal information. Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2024} similarly combined resting-state functional connectivity with phenotypic data. A recent preprint has also explored 3D structural MRI and clinical phenotypic information using adaptive gated multimodal fusion, reporting 87\% accuracy on the Peking-1 subset of ADHD-200.% TODO: add \cite{...} once the 2026 preprint's bibliographic details are verified; remove this sentence if they cannot be.
 In contrast, the present work focuses specifically on reducing model complexity through depthwise-separable 3D convolution and lightweight channel attention while evaluating a multisite cohort.

\subsection{Lightweight and Parameter-Efficient ADHD Models}
Model complexity is an important consideration when deep learning systems are intended for computationally constrained environments. Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2025} explicitly investigated parameter efficiency for ADHD classification and proposed an attention-driven KAN architecture with only a few thousand parameters. Following this direction, the present study treats \emph{parameter efficiency as a primary design objective} rather than a secondary consideration. ADHDNet uses depthwise-separable 3D convolution to reduce the number of learnable parameters while retaining volumetric processing capability.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Existing studies have demonstrated the value of multimodal information and, separately, of parameter-efficient model design for ADHD classification. However, comparatively few studies combine both directions by processing volumetric structural MRI together with phenotypic information under an explicit parameter budget. ADHDNet is designed to address this combination.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

Experiments were conducted using the preprocessed anatomical component of the publicly available ADHD-200 dataset~\cite{adhd200consortium2012,bellec2017}. The dataset initially contained 575 successfully processed subjects (353 controls and 222 ADHD) distributed across six sites: KKI, NYU, OHSU, Peking\_1, Pittsburgh, and NeuroIMAGE. The Pittsburgh site contained 78 control subjects but no ADHD subjects. Since this site does not provide both diagnostic classes required for binary discrimination, it was excluded from model development. After this site-level filtering, the final cohort consisted of 497 subjects (222 ADHD and 275 controls). The distribution after site exclusion is shown in Table~\ref{tab:dataset}.

\subsection{MRI Preprocessing}

The structural MRI data were provided in NIfTI format. To obtain a computationally manageable input representation, each MRI volume was resized to $64\times64\times64$ voxels using trilinear interpolation. Each resulting volume was independently normalized using min-max normalization,
\begin{equation}
x' = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}},
\end{equation}
such that voxel intensities were mapped to the range $[0,1]$. A relatively simple preprocessing pipeline was adopted to maintain a compact and reproducible experimental setup. No additional skull stripping, atlas-based segmentation, N4 bias correction, or anatomical segmentation was applied.

\subsection{Phenotypic Features}

Five phenotypic variables were incorporated: age, sex, medication status, site, and ADHD Index. Missing values represented by \texttt{-999} were converted to missing values before imputation. Continuous variables were normalized, while categorical variables were numerically encoded. The resulting features were passed through a compact multilayer perceptron (Sect.~\ref{sec:pheno}) to obtain a low-dimensional phenotypic representation complementary to the MRI representation.

\section{Proposed ADHDNet}
%======================================================================

ADHDNet consists of a lightweight 3D MRI encoder, a compact phenotypic encoder, and an adaptive gated fusion module (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). A $1\times64\times64\times64$ sMRI volume is processed through three depthwise-separable Conv3D blocks with channel progression $24\rightarrow48\rightarrow96$, each followed by max pooling; a single 3D ECA module then recalibrates the final feature map before global average pooling and projection to a 128-dimensional MRI representation. In parallel, five phenotypic features are encoded by a $5\rightarrow32\rightarrow32$ MLP. A gating network computes fusion weights from the concatenated 160-dimensional vector of the MRI and phenotypic representations, the phenotypic representation is projected to 128 dimensions, and the two 128-dimensional representations are combined by gated element-wise fusion and passed to a $128\rightarrow64\rightarrow2$ classification head. The complete model contains 71,552 trainable parameters. Each component is described below.

The phenotypic branch encodes five features (age, sex, medication status, site, and ADHD Index) with a $5\rightarrow32\rightarrow32$ MLP and a $32\rightarrow128$ projection to obtain $z_P$. \subsection{Lightweight 3D MRI Encoder}

The imaging branch consists of three depthwise-separable 3D convolutional blocks with channel progression $1\rightarrow24\rightarrow48\rightarrow96$. Each block consists of a depthwise-separable Conv3D operation ($3\times3\times3$ depthwise kernel followed by a $1\times1\times1$ pointwise convolution), batch normalization, ReLU activation, and $2\times2\times2$ max pooling, so that the spatial resolution is halved by each block ($64^3\rightarrow32^3\rightarrow16^3\rightarrow8^3$). Depthwise-separable convolution~\cite{chollet2017xception,howard2017mobilenets} decomposes a conventional convolution into (i) a depthwise spatial convolution and (ii) a pointwise $1\times1\times1$ convolution. For a conventional 3D convolution with kernel size $K$, the parameter count is approximately
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{conv}} = K^3 C_{in} C_{out},
\end{equation}
whereas depthwise-separable convolution requires approximately
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{DS}} = K^3 C_{in} + C_{in} C_{out}.
\end{equation}
Since the second expression can be considerably smaller when $C_{out}$ is large, depthwise-separable convolution provides a mechanism for reducing the number of trainable parameters. After the third block, a single 3D ECA module (Sect.~\ref{sec:eca}) recalibrates the $96\times8\times8\times8$ feature map, and adaptive global average pooling produces a 96-dimensional representation, which is projected to a 128-dimensional MRI embedding.

\subsection{Efficient Channel Attention}
\label{sec:eca}

Following volumetric feature extraction, a single 3D ECA module is applied to the output of the third encoder block, recalibrating the 96 learned feature channels before global pooling. To improve feature recalibration without introducing a large parameter overhead, a three-dimensional extension of efficient channel attention (ECA)~\cite{wang2020eca} is used. The attention operation estimates channel importance from globally pooled feature responses and applies channel-wise weights to the volumetric feature maps:
\begin{equation}
F_{att} = A(F) \odot F,
\end{equation}
where $F$ represents the feature tensor and $A(F)$ the learned channel attention weights. Following ECA-Net~\cite{wang2020eca}, $A(F)$ is obtained by global average pooling, a 1D convolution across the channel dimension whose kernel size is chosen adaptively from the number of channels ($k=3$ for 96 channels), and a sigmoid. The module therefore adds only three parameters while allowing the network to emphasize informative feature channels.

\subsection{Phenotypic Encoder}
\label{sec:pheno}

In parallel, the phenotypic branch encodes the five tabular variables into a compact representation. The phenotype encoder is a small multilayer perceptron with dimensions $5\rightarrow32\rightarrow32$. ReLU activation, batch normalization~\cite{ioffe2015bn}, and dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout} are used to regularize the representation. The resulting 32-dimensional phenotype representation is used directly by the gating network and is separately projected to 128 dimensions for element-wise fusion (Sect.~\ref{sec:fusion}).

\subsection{Adaptive Gated Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

The two modality-specific embeddings are subsequently integrated using adaptive gated fusion. Simple concatenation treats both modalities equally at the fusion stage; instead, ADHDNet employs a learnable gating mechanism, in the spirit of gated multimodal units~\cite{arevalo2017gmu}. The MRI branch produces a 128-dimensional representation $z_{MRI}\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$, while the phenotypic MLP produces a 32-dimensional representation $h_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$. These representations are concatenated to form a 160-dimensional vector $z=[z_{MRI};h_P]\in\mathbb{R}^{160}$, from which the gating network generates
\begin{equation}
g = \sigma\!\left(W_2\,\delta(W_1 z)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{128},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid function and $\delta$ a nonlinear activation. The phenotypic representation is independently projected to 128 dimensions, $z_P = \mathrm{Proj}(h_P) \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$, and the fused representation is computed by gated element-wise fusion:
\begin{equation}
z_F = g \odot z_{MRI} + (1-g) \odot z_P .
\end{equation}
Thus, the model can dynamically determine the relative contribution of the imaging and phenotypic representations for each sample.

\subsection{Classification Head}

The fused 128-dimensional representation is passed through a compact classifier with dimensions $128\rightarrow64\rightarrow2$, whose two output units correspond to the ADHD and control classes. Softmax probabilities are used to obtain the predicted class.

\section{Model Complexity}
%======================================================================

Parameter efficiency is a central design consideration of ADHDNet. The proposed model contains 71,552 trainable parameters ($\approx$0.0716M). For comparison, a conventional Conv3D multimodal model was implemented under the same experimental framework. It uses four standard (non-separable) Conv3D blocks with channel progression $32\rightarrow64\rightarrow128\rightarrow256$, 3D ECA after blocks 2--4, generalized-mean pooling, a 256-dimensional MRI embedding, a deeper $5\rightarrow32\rightarrow64\rightarrow64$ phenotypic MLP, the same gated fusion mechanism, and a $256\rightarrow128\rightarrow64\rightarrow2$ classification head. This comparator contains 1,376,080 parameters ($\approx$1.376M). The relative reduction is
\begin{equation}
\frac{1{,}376{,}080 - 71{,}552}{1{,}376{,}080}\times 100 \approx 94.8\%.
\end{equation}
Stored as 32-bit floating-point values, the trained weights of ADHDNet occupy $71{,}552\times4 = 286{,}208$ bytes ($\approx$0.27\,MiB), compared with $1{,}376{,}080\times4 = 5{,}504{,}320$ bytes ($\approx$5.25\,MiB) for the comparator. These figures describe FP32 weight storage only, not runtime memory usage. FLOPs and inference latency are reported in Sect.~\ref{sec:efficiency}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Training Strategy}
The network was trained in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} using the AdamW optimizer~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with a learning rate of $1\times10^{-4}$, weight decay of $1\times10^{-4}$, batch size of 8, a maximum of 150 epochs, early stopping with patience 15, label smoothing~\cite{szegedy2016inception} of 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0, and cosine annealing learning-rate scheduling~\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr}. Weighted cross-entropy loss was used to account for class imbalance, with class weights computed from the training subjects. During training, each MRI volume was randomly flipped along each spatial axis with probability 0.5. The checkpoint with the highest validation AUC was retained. The random seed was fixed to 42; Python, NumPy, and PyTorch random states were seeded, and deterministic CUDA settings were used where applicable.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
Three complementary evaluation settings were used.

\emph{Held-out validation.} The final dataset was divided using a stratified 80:20 train--validation split (random seed 42), giving 397 training subjects and 100 held-out validation subjects (45 ADHD and 55 controls). Imputation statistics (median for continuous and mode for categorical variables) and normalization statistics were computed from the training subjects only and applied unchanged to the validation subjects. The validation set was used for early stopping and model selection; the reported results should therefore be interpreted as \emph{held-out validation performance} rather than independent external test performance.

\emph{Cross-validation.} To assess the variability of performance across data partitions, ADHDNet and the conventional Conv3D comparator were evaluated with stratified 5-fold cross-validation on all 497 subjects (shuffled with seed 42). In each fold, imputation and normalization statistics were fitted on that fold's training subjects only. Each model was trained for at most 60 epochs with early stopping (patience 10) and otherwise identical settings, and fold metrics were taken at the epoch with the highest validation AUC.

\emph{Ablation and efficiency.} Component-wise ablation variants were trained and evaluated on the same held-out split and with the same training budget as the full model (Sect.~\ref{sec:ablation}). Computational cost was measured for a single $1\times64\times64\times64$ volume with five phenotypic features: FLOPs were counted with PyTorch's \texttt{FlopCounterMode}, and inference latency was averaged over 50 timed forward passes after 10 warm-up passes on a CUDA GPU, at batch sizes 1 and 8.% TODO: name the GPU model used for the latency benchmark

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
With ADHD as the positive class, the model was evaluated using
\begin{gather}
\text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}, \quad
\text{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}, \\
\text{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}, \quad
\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \\
\text{NPV}=\frac{TN}{TN+FN}, \quad
F_1 = 2\,\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}.
\end{gather}
Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was also performed and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated.

\subsection{Per-Class Error Analysis}

The confusion matrix in Fig.~\ref{fig:roc_cm}(b) shows how the errors are distributed between the two classes. ADHDNet correctly classified 43 of 45 ADHD subjects and 47 of 55 controls. Of the 10 misclassifications, 8 were controls predicted as ADHD and 2 were ADHD subjects predicted as controls. The model therefore errs more often towards false positives than false negatives, which is reflected in the higher sensitivity (95.56\%) than specificity (85.45\%) and in the higher NPV (95.92\%) than precision (84.31\%). On this validation cohort, a negative prediction was consequently more reliable than a positive one.

\subsection{Cross-Validation}

Table~\ref{tab:cv} reports stratified 5-fold cross-validation results for ADHDNet and the conventional Conv3D comparator, and Fig.~\ref{fig:cv} shows the per-fold AUC values. ADHDNet achieved a mean accuracy of $87.52\pm4.61\%$ and a mean AUC of $0.9512\pm0.0169$, with per-fold accuracy ranging from 79.80\% to 90.91\%. The comparator achieved a mean accuracy of $86.51\pm3.64\%$ and a mean AUC of $0.9566\pm0.0197$. The two models therefore reached comparable discrimination, with a mean AUC difference of 0.005, although ADHDNet uses 19.2$\times$ fewer parameters. ADHDNet obtained a slightly higher mean accuracy, sensitivity, and F1-score, and its sensitivity varied less across folds ($\pm5.75\%$ versus $\pm9.63\%$). The held-out accuracy of 90.00\% lies at the upper end of the cross-validated range; the cross-validated mean of 87.52\% is therefore the more representative estimate of expected performance.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}
\label{sec:efficiency}

Because parameter efficiency is a primary design objective of ADHDNet, its computational cost was measured against the conventional Conv3D comparator (Table~\ref{tab:efficiency}, Fig.~\ref{fig:efficiency}). ADHDNet contains 71,552 trainable parameters compared with 1,376,080 for the comparator, a 94.8\% reduction (19.2$\times$), and its FP32 weight storage falls from approximately 5.25\,MiB to 0.27\,MiB. It requires 0.1932 GFLOPs per volume compared with 6.7952 GFLOPs, a 97.2\% reduction (35.2$\times$). On the GPU used for benchmarking, single-volume inference latency fell from $4.32\pm0.12$\,ms to $2.36\pm0.15$\,ms (1.8$\times$ faster), and batched throughput at batch size 8 rose from 242.6 to 662.9 volumes per second (2.7$\times$). Latency and throughput depend on hardware, whereas parameter and FLOP counts do not; the speed-up is smaller than the FLOP reduction because small 3D models do not fully utilize the GPU. Together with the cross-validation results, these measurements show that ADHDNet reaches discrimination comparable to the much larger comparator at a small fraction of its computational cost.

The phenotypic branch had by far the largest effect. Without it (A2), accuracy fell to 54.00\% and AUC to 0.5644, close to chance level, indicating that under the present preprocessing the MRI branch alone provides little discriminative information and that most of the model's performance derives from the phenotypic features. Removing ECA (A1) reduced accuracy by 3.0 percentage points and AUC by 0.0056, a consistent but small change for a module with three parameters. Replacing gated fusion with concatenation (A3) gave a slightly lower accuracy and F1-score but a slightly higher AUC (0.9564), so this split provides no clear evidence that gating outperforms concatenation. Replacing depthwise-separable with standard convolutions (A4) produced essentially the same performance (89.00\% accuracy, AUC 0.9547) with 3.1$\times$ more parameters, confirming that the depthwise-separable factorization reduces model size without loss of accuracy. Because these comparisons use a single split of 100 subjects, where one subject corresponds to one percentage point of accuracy, differences of a few points should be interpreted with caution.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing Studies}

Existing studies illustrate several directions in ADHD classification, including conventional machine learning using structural MRI-derived features, unimodal deep learning and graph-based models of functional MRI, multimodal approaches, and lightweight models (Table~\ref{tab:literature}). Among multimodal studies, Lohani and Rana~\cite{lohani2023} reported 75\% accuracy using structural MRI-derived features and personal characteristics with conventional classifiers, while Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2024} reported 77.59\% using resting-state functional connectivity and phenotypic information. Among unimodal studies, Gülhan and Özmen~\cite{gulhan2025} applied a 3D CNN to regional fMRI analysis on ADHD-200 and reported a best per-site accuracy of 76.19\%, Zamanzadeh et al.~\cite{zamanzadeh2024} reported 74.30\% using graph features of audiovisual integration networks with an EasyEnsemble classifier, and Wu et al.~\cite{wu2025hagcn} reported 77.95\% using the HAGCN graph convolutional framework. More recently, parameter-efficient learning has been investigated using attention-driven KANs, with Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2025} reporting 79.25\% accuracy using a model with only a few thousand parameters.

These studies provide useful evidence for both multimodal learning and parameter-efficient modeling, but their imaging modalities, datasets, and evaluation protocols differ from those used in the present study; the reported accuracies are therefore not directly comparable. ADHDNet instead focuses on combining volumetric structural MRI and phenotypic information within a compact 3D CNN architecture.

\section{Discussion}
%======================================================================

The results show that a compact 3D CNN can jointly model structural MRI and phenotypic information at a small fraction of the cost of a conventional 3D model. ADHDNet contains 71,552 trainable parameters and requires 0.19 GFLOPs per volume, 94.8\% fewer parameters and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs than the conventional Conv3D comparator, while reaching comparable cross-validated discrimination (AUC $0.951\pm0.017$ versus $0.957\pm0.020$) and a slightly higher mean accuracy ($87.52\%$ versus $86.51\%$).

The ablation study clarifies where this efficiency comes from and which components matter. Depthwise-separable convolution reduced the parameter count by a factor of 3.1 relative to an otherwise identical standard-convolution model without loss of accuracy, and the single 3D ECA module improved accuracy by three percentage points at a cost of three parameters. Gated fusion, in contrast, performed on par with concatenation on this split, so its benefit over simpler fusion remains to be established.

The most important finding of the ablation, however, is the dominance of the phenotypic branch: the MRI-only variant performed close to chance, whereas every variant that retained the phenotypic branch achieved an AUC above 0.94. The phenotypic input includes the ADHD Index, a clinical symptom rating that is closely related to the diagnostic label itself, and site, which is correlated with class proportions in ADHD-200. The reported accuracy should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that structural MRI alone, as represented by ADHDNet, discriminates ADHD from controls. Nor should the 90\% held-out accuracy be read as evidence that ADHDNet is superior to previously published approaches, since those studies use different modalities, preprocessing, and protocols. The main contribution is instead a \emph{parameter-efficient multimodal 3D architecture} that combines depthwise-separable volumetric convolution and lightweight channel attention with adaptive fusion of structural MRI and phenotypic representations, together with an evaluation that makes the relative contributions of its components explicit.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Several limitations should be considered. First, performance depends predominantly on the phenotypic features, in particular potentially on the ADHD Index; future work should evaluate the model without the ADHD Index and site to measure the diagnostic value of the MRI representation itself. Second, the held-out results come from a single split that was also used for early stopping and model selection, and the cross-validation and ablation metrics were likewise taken at the epoch of best validation AUC; none of these results constitute an independent external test. Third, although the dataset spans multiple acquisition sites, cross-site generalization was not explicitly evaluated, for example with leave-one-site-out validation. Fourth, the ablation used a single split without repeated runs or statistical testing, so small differences between variants are not conclusive. Fifth, the simple preprocessing pipeline, which resizes the provided volumes to $64^3$ without registration or atlas-based segmentation, may limit the anatomical information available to the MRI branch. Finally, latency was measured on a single GPU and the work addresses only binary ADHD-versus-control classification, not ADHD subtypes.

\section{Conclusion}
%======================================================================

This paper presented ADHDNet, a lightweight multimodal 3D CNN for ADHD classification using structural MRI and phenotypic information. The architecture combines depthwise-separable 3D convolution, a single efficient channel attention module, a compact phenotypic encoder, and adaptive gated fusion. On a multisite subset of ADHD-200 comprising 497 subjects, ADHDNet achieved 90.00\% accuracy and an AUC of 0.9543 on a held-out validation set and $87.52\pm4.61\%$ accuracy in 5-fold cross-validation, using 71,552 trainable parameters and 0.19 GFLOPs per volume. Compared with a conventional Conv3D comparator, this corresponds to 94.8\% fewer parameters and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs at comparable cross-validated AUC. The ablation study showed that the phenotypic branch supplies most of the discriminative information, with the MRI-only variant performing close to chance. Future work will evaluate the model without the ADHD Index and site, investigate preprocessing that strengthens the MRI representation, and perform cross-site and independent external validation.
