\begin{abstract}
ADHD is diagnosed mainly through behavioral and clinical assessment, which has led to interest in neuroimaging-based methods. Deep learning models for structural MRI, however, often use large 3D networks, and multimodal models add further cost through fusion components. This study examines whether similar predictive performance can be obtained with a much smaller multimodal 3D model. For this purpose, ADHDNet is proposed: a lightweight 3D CNN that combines depthwise-separable convolutions and efficient channel attention with a gated fusion of MRI and phenotypic features. On 497 ADHD-200 subjects from five sites, an accuracy of 90.00\% and an AUC of 0.9543 were obtained by ADHDNet (71,552 parameters; 0.19 GFLOPs per volume) on a held-out validation set, and $87.52\pm4.61\%$ accuracy (AUC $0.951\pm0.017$) was obtained in 5-fold cross-validation. Compared with a conventional Conv3D multimodal model, 94.8\% fewer parameters and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs were required at a similar cross-validated AUC. However, close-to-chance performance was observed for the MRI-only variant in an ablation study. The results show that similar multimodal performance can be obtained with a much smaller 3D model, but also that this performance comes mainly from the phenotypic features, so the additional contribution of structural MRI remains unclear.

\keywords{ADHD \and Structural MRI \and Multimodal learning \and 3D CNN \and Lightweight deep learning}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder marked by persistent inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. It is diagnosed mainly through behavioral and clinical assessment, which can be difficult because its symptoms overlap with those of other developmental and psychiatric conditions. This has motivated work on neuroimaging-based methods that look for brain patterns associated with ADHD~\cite{zhangjames2023,tian2024review}.

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) shows brain anatomy without radiation and has been widely used for this purpose~\cite{tian2024review}. In early studies, conventional classifiers were applied to measures such as gray-matter volume and cortical thickness~\cite{lohani2023}. Deep learning later made it possible to learn features directly from the images, and recent ADHD studies have used convolutional, transformer-based, graph-based, and multimodal models~\cite{firouzi2024,gulhan2025,wu2025hagcn,sorour2026}. Multimodal models also add phenotypic features such as age, sex, and clinical ratings. As these models grow deeper and wider and use more complex fusion, the computational and memory cost rises. This matters when computing resources are limited, and compact models, such as the few-thousand-parameter network of Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2025}, have been explored for this reason.

Three questions follow from this. Can structural MRI and phenotypic features be modeled together at a much lower computational cost? Can a small 3D model keep the performance of a larger one? And does the resulting performance come from the MRI, from the phenotypic features, or from particular model components? No new building blocks are proposed. Instead, established efficient components are combined in a model called \emph{ADHDNet}, and this combination is tested under controlled conditions. The main contributions of this study are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{lightweight multimodal 3D model} that learns from MRI and phenotypic features together, with far fewer parameters and FLOPs than a conventional Conv3D multimodal model.
  \item A \textbf{lightweight MRI encoder} that combines depthwise-separable 3D convolutions with efficient channel attention to keep 3D feature extraction while reducing the number of parameters.
  \item A \textbf{sample-specific gated fusion} of the two modalities, together with a controlled comparison against simple concatenation.
  \item A \textbf{systematic evaluation} of performance, model size, computational cost, and the role of each component, using a held-out set, 5-fold cross-validation, and ablation experiments.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
%======================================================================

\subsection{MRI-Based ADHD Classification}
In early MRI-based studies of ADHD, handcrafted features were used with conventional classifiers~\cite{zhangjames2023,tian2024review}. For example, Lohani and Rana~\cite{lohani2023} combined gray-matter volume and cortical-thickness features with personal characteristics and reported 75\% accuracy with repeated ten-fold cross\-validation. Later work learns features directly from the data. CNNs have been applied to fMRI~\cite{firouzi2024,gulhan2025}, graph-based models of resting-state networks have been combined with ensemble classifiers or attention~\cite{zamanzadeh2024,wu2025hagcn}. Recent structural MRI studies have explored transformer and representation-learning approaches. Sorour et al.~\cite{sorour2026} combined a Vision Transformer with an enhanced CNN on raw and transformed MRI representations and reported 99.4\% accuracy, but with 86.19M parameters. Maeda et al.~\cite{maeda2026} applied a Vision Transformer with a gross-region grid to individual-space T1 images (AUC 75.0\%), and Gonz\'alez et al.~\cite{gonzalez2026} quantified morphological alterations with a curvelet-based sMRI representation (balanced accuracy 78.0\%). These studies show that ADHD-related features can be learned from neuroimaging data. The focus, however, is on accuracy: parameter count, FLOPs, memory use, and latency are rarely reported, so it is hard to judge whether such 3D models can run with limited computing resources.

\subsection{Multimodal ADHD Classification}
Phenotypic features such as age, sex, medication status, and clinical ratings can carry information that the images do not. Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2024} combined resting-state functional connectivity with phenotypic data and reported that adding the phenotypic data raised accuracy to 77.59\%. Neither Maeda et al.~\cite{maeda2026} nor Gonz\'alez et al.~\cite{gonzalez2026} fuses phenotypic features with the imaging data.
 These studies suggest that phenotypic features can complement imaging, but the contribution of each modality is usually not separated within the same model. A gain attributed to multimodal learning may therefore come from the predictive strength of the phenotypic features rather than from extra information in the images.

\subsection{Lightweight Models}
Depthwise-separable convolution~\cite{chollet2017xception} splits spatial filtering and channel mixing into two steps and is a standard way to reduce the number of parameters in a CNN. Efficient channel attention (ECA)~\cite{wang2020eca} reweights feature channels using very few parameters. In ADHD classification, compact models have so far worked on derived features rather than 3D images~\cite{deepika2025}. To the best of the author's knowledge, these techniques have not been combined in a small 3D multimodal model for ADHD with the computational cost measured explicitly.

\subsection{Adaptive Multimodal Fusion}
Concatenation combines the features of each modality in the same way for every sample. Gated multimodal units~\cite{arevalo2017gmu} instead learn gates that weight each modality per sample. No controlled test of whether such gating helps over concatenation for MRI and phenotypic data in ADHD appears to have been reported. This is therefore treated as an open question rather than assumed.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Existing ADHD studies (Table~\ref{tab:literature}) show the value of neuroimaging, phenotypic features, and deep learning. However, the computational cost and the contribution of each modality and model component are rarely reported. Three questions remain open: (i)~can structural MRI and phenotypic features be modeled together with a much smaller 3D model; (ii)~can a lightweight 3D model maintain performance similar to a conventional 3D model; and (iii)~which model components and modalities actually contribute to the performance? ADHDNet addresses questions (i) and (ii) through its compact design (contributions 1--2), and question (iii) through controlled comparisons and ablation (contributions 3--4).

\subsection{Dataset}
The preprocessed anatomical data of the public ADHD-200 dataset~\cite{adhd200consortium2012,bellec2017} were used. The dataset contains 575 successfully processed subjects (222 ADHD, 353 controls) from six sites. The Pittsburgh site had 78 controls and no ADHD subjects. Because it does not include both classes, it was excluded. The final cohort therefore has 497 subjects (222 ADHD, 275 controls) from five sites (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}).

\subsection{Preprocessing and Phenotypic Features}
Each NIfTI volume was resized to $64\times64\times64$ voxels with trilinear interpolation and min-max normalized to $[0,1]$. To keep the pipeline simple and reproducible, no additional skull stripping, registration, bias correction, or segmentation was applied. Five phenotypic features were used: age, sex, medication status, acquisition site, and ADHD Index. The ADHD Index is a clinical symptom score that is closely related to the diagnostic label; it is treated as a phenotypic feature, not as an imaging feature. Missing values were filled with the training-set median (continuous) or mode (categorical). Age and ADHD Index were z-scored with training-set statistics, and categorical features were scaled to $[0,1]$.

\section{Proposed ADHDNet}
%======================================================================

To enable efficient learning of discriminative representations from structural MRI while incorporating complementary phenotypic information, this study proposes ADHDNet (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}), a lightweight multimodal 3D deep learning framework for ADHD classification. The framework jointly learns MRI representations and phenotypic representations and adaptively integrates them through a gated fusion mechanism. With only 71,552 trainable parameters, ADHDNet is designed to provide an efficient alternative to conventional 3D CNN-based multimodal models. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, the proposed architecture consists of four main components: a lightweight 3D MRI encoder, a phenotypic encoder, an adaptive gated fusion module, and a classification head.

\paragraph{Lightweight 3D MRI Encoder.}
In 3D CNNs, most parameters and computation come from the convolutions, because a standard 3D convolution with kernel size $K$ needs $K^3C_{in}C_{out}$ weights. Let $X\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times64\times64\times64}$ be the normalized sMRI volume. The encoder passes $X$ through three blocks that reduce the resolution and increase the number of channels ($24\rightarrow48\rightarrow96$ channels; $64^3\rightarrow8^3$). Each block uses a $3\times3\times3$ depthwise convolution followed by a $1\times1\times1$ pointwise convolution~\cite{chollet2017xception}, then batch normalization, ReLU, and $2\times2\times2$ max pooling:
\begin{equation}
H_{l} = \mathrm{MaxPool}\big(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{BN}(\mathrm{PW}_l(\mathrm{DW}_l(H_{l-1}))))\big), \quad H_0 = X.
\end{equation}
Each block then needs only $K^3C_{in}+C_{in}C_{out}$ weights. This design keeps the 3D structure of the MRI while reducing the number of parameters. Its effect is evaluated in ablation A4.

\paragraph{3D Efficient Channel Attention.}
Not all feature channels are equally useful, but standard attention modules add fully connected layers that are costly in a small model. To test whether a very light form of channel attention helps, a 3D version of ECA~\cite{wang2020eca} is applied to the encoder output $F=H_3\in\mathbb{R}^{96\times8^3}$:
\begin{equation}
F_{att} = \sigma\big(\mathrm{Conv1D}_k(\mathrm{GAP}(F))\big)\odot F,
\end{equation}
where GAP is global average pooling. The 1D convolution across channels has a kernel size chosen from the number of channels ($k=3$ for 96 channels), so the module adds only three parameters. $F_{att}$ is then average-pooled and projected to the MRI embedding $z_{MRI}\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$. Its contribution is evaluated by removing ECA in ablation A1.

\paragraph{Phenotypic Encoder and Gated Fusion.}
The two modalities may not contribute equally for every subject. A learnable gating function is therefore used to adjust the contribution of each modality per subject. A $5\rightarrow32\rightarrow32$ MLP with batch normalization, ReLU, and dropout maps the phenotypic features to $h_P\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$. Following gated multimodal units~\cite{arevalo2017gmu}, a gating network computes a gate vector $g$ from the 160-dimensional concatenation $[z_{MRI};h_P]$, and $h_P$ is projected to the phenotype embedding $z_P\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$:
\begin{equation}
g=\sigma\big(W_2\,\delta(W_1[z_{MRI};h_P])\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{128}, \qquad z_F = g \odot z_{MRI} + (1-g) \odot z_P,
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the ReLU. A $128\rightarrow64\rightarrow2$ head with dropout maps $z_F$ to class probabilities. Ablation A3 replaces the gate with concatenation, and A2 removes the phenotypic branch.

\section{Experimental Setup}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Training.}
All models were implemented in PyTorch and trained with AdamW (learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batch size 8, at most 150 epochs with early stopping (patience 15), class-weighted cross-entropy with label smoothing of 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0, and cosine annealing. Training volumes were randomly flipped along each axis ($p=0.5$). The checkpoint with the best validation AUC was kept, and all seeds were fixed to 42.

\paragraph{Comparator.}
As a reference, a conventional Conv3D multimodal model was built in the same code base. It uses the same preprocessing, data splits, fusion mechanism, training settings, and evaluation, so differences in cost and performance come mainly from its larger 3D backbone and embeddings. It has four standard Conv3D blocks ($32\rightarrow64\rightarrow128\rightarrow256$ channels) with 3D ECA after blocks 2--4, generalized-mean pooling, a 256-dimensional embedding, a $5\rightarrow32\rightarrow64\rightarrow64$ phenotypic MLP, the same gated fusion, and a $256\rightarrow128\rightarrow64\rightarrow2$ head (1,376,080 parameters).

\paragraph{Evaluation Protocol.}
(i)~\emph{Held-out validation:} a stratified 80:20 split gave 397 training and 100 validation subjects (45 ADHD, 55 controls). Because this validation set was also used for early stopping and model selection, these results are held-out validation results, not independent test results. (ii)~\emph{Cross-validation:} to check whether the held-out result is representative, both models were evaluated with stratified 5-fold cross-validation on all 497 subjects (at most 60 epochs, patience 10; metrics at the best-AUC epoch). (iii)~\emph{Ablation:} to measure the effect of depthwise-separable convolution, ECA, the phenotypic branch, and gated fusion, each variant changes one component and keeps everything else the same, including the data split. Imputation and normalization statistics were always computed on the training subjects only, and ADHD was the positive class. FLOPs were counted with PyTorch's \texttt{FlopCounterMode} for one $64^3$ volume, and latency was averaged over 50 runs after 10 warm-up runs on a CUDA GPU.% TODO: name the GPU model used for the latency benchmark

\subsection{Held-Out Classification Performance}
On the held-out validation set (Table~\ref{tab:results}), an accuracy of 90.00\% and an AUC of 0.9543 were obtained by ADHDNet. The best validation AUC occurred at epoch 60, and training stopped at epoch 75. In total, 43 of 45 ADHD subjects and 47 of 55 controls were correctly classified (Fig.~\ref{fig:roc_cm}), with 8 false positives and 2 false negatives. Sensitivity was 95.56\%, specificity 85.45\%, precision 84.31\%, and NPV 95.92\%.

\subsection{Cross-Validation and Computational Cost}
Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares ADHDNet with the Conv3D comparator. In 5-fold cross-validation, ADHDNet obtained $87.52\pm4.61\%$ accuracy (per-fold range 79.80--90.91\%) and an AUC of $0.9512\pm0.0169$, compared with $86.51\pm3.64\%$ and $0.9566\pm0.0197$ for the comparator. The two models performed similarly: ADHDNet had slightly higher mean accuracy, sensitivity, and F1-score, but a slightly lower mean AUC. The held-out accuracy of 90.00\% is at the upper end of the cross-validation range, so 87.52\% is the more representative estimate. With ADHDNet, the number of parameters was reduced by 94.8\% (19.2$\times$) and the FLOPs by 97.2\% (35.2$\times$), and the FP32 weight storage was reduced from 5.25 to 0.27\,MiB (Fig.~\ref{fig:cv_eff_abl}a). Latency was 1.8 times lower and batched throughput 2.7 times higher. A much smaller 3D backbone therefore did not reduce multimodal performance here. As the ablation shows, however, this performance does not come mainly from the MRI.

The ablation variants (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, Fig.~\ref{fig:cv_eff_abl}b) remove ECA (A1), remove the phenotypic branch (A2), replace gated fusion with concatenation (A3), or use standard convolutions (A4). Removing the phenotypic branch reduced performance to near chance (AUC 0.5644). The other variants changed accuracy by at most 3 points. With 100 validation subjects, one subject equals one percentage point, so small differences should be read with caution.

\section{Discussion}
%======================================================================

Cross-validated performance similar to that of the much larger comparator was obtained with ADHDNet. The ablation, however, shows that this performance comes mainly from the phenotypic features rather than from structural MRI.
Using standard instead of depthwise-separable convolutions (A4) tripled the number of parameters but did not improve accuracy or AUC on this split. The extra convolutional capacity was therefore not useful in this setting. Removing ECA (A1) lowered accuracy by 3.0 points, which is three validation subjects. ECA is best seen as a cheap refinement whose benefit still needs to be confirmed with repeated runs. Without the phenotypic branch (A2), performance was close to chance, while every variant that kept it reached an AUC above 0.94. Gated fusion did not clearly outperform concatenation (A3), so its benefit is not yet established.
The phenotypic features include the ADHD Index, a symptom score closely related to the diagnosis, and site, whose class proportions differ strongly (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). The reported accuracy therefore does not show that structural MRI, as used here, can distinguish ADHD from controls. It should also not be read as clinically usable diagnostic performance. ADHDNet's accuracy is numerically higher than the values in Table~\ref{tab:literature}, but those studies use different modalities and protocols. The main point of this study is therefore not higher accuracy. It is the combination of a compact 3D multimodal model with a controlled analysis of its cost and of what each component contributes.

\section{Conclusion}
%======================================================================

This study investigated whether structural MRI and phenotypic features can be modeled together with a much smaller 3D model. Cross-validated performance similar to that of a conventional Conv3D multimodal model was obtained with ADHDNet, while 94.8\% fewer parameters and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs were required. The ablation showed that depthwise-separable convolution gave most of the reduction in model size, ECA gave only a small gain, and gated fusion did not clearly outperform concatenation. Most importantly, removing the phenotypic branch reduced performance to near chance, so the reported performance is driven mainly by the phenotypic features. The additional contribution of structural MRI therefore remains unclear. Future work should remove features that are close to the label, such as the ADHD Index and site, and test generalization across sites and on external cohorts.
