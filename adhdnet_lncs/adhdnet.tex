\begin{abstract}
Diagnosis of Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) relies primarily on behavioral and clinical assessment, motivating neuroimaging-based computational approaches; however, many deep learning models for neuroimaging have substantial parameter and computational requirements. This work presents \emph{ADHDNet}, a lightweight multimodal 3D convolutional neural network that combines structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) with phenotypic information. ADHDNet uses depthwise-separable 3D convolutions, a single 3D efficient channel attention module, and adaptive gated fusion of MRI and phenotypic representations. On a multisite ADHD-200 cohort of 497 subjects, with volumes resized to $64\times64\times64$ and five phenotypic features, ADHDNet (71,552 parameters; 0.19 GFLOPs) achieved 90.00\% accuracy and an AUC of 0.9543 on a stratified held-out validation set and $87.52\pm4.61\%$ accuracy (AUC $0.951\pm0.017$) in 5-fold cross-validation. Compared with a conventional Conv3D multimodal model (1.376M parameters), it uses 94.8\% fewer parameters and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs at comparable cross-validated AUC ($0.957\pm0.020$). A component-wise ablation shows, however, that the phenotypic branch supplies most of the discriminative information, as the MRI-only variant performed close to chance.

\keywords{ADHD \and Structural MRI \and Multimodal learning \and 3D CNN \and Depthwise-separable convolution \and Lightweight deep learning}
\end{abstract}

%======================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. Its diagnosis relies primarily on behavioral and clinical evaluation, which is challenging because ADHD symptoms overlap with those of other developmental and psychiatric conditions. This has motivated neuroimaging-based computational approaches for identifying brain patterns associated with ADHD~\cite{zhangjames2023,tian2024review,lohani2025review}.

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) provides non-invasive information about brain anatomy and has been widely used for computational ADHD analysis~\cite{chen2025meta}. Earlier studies relied on manually derived measurements, such as gray-matter volume and cortical thickness, combined with personal characteristics; Lohani and Rana~\cite{lohani2023}, for example, reported a maximum accuracy of 75\% with conventional classifiers. Deep learning has since enabled direct learning of imaging representations, with recent ADHD studies exploring convolutional, multimodal, attention-based, and ensemble architectures~\cite{firouzi2024,hu2024,cao2024,pedrollo2026}. Increasing architectural complexity, however, also increases the number of trainable parameters and the computational cost, which makes parameter efficiency an important consideration for resource-constrained settings. Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2025} explicitly addressed this with a lightweight attention-driven KAN that reached 79.25\% accuracy with only a few thousand parameters.

Relatively little work has investigated parameter-efficient multimodal architectures that directly process volumetric structural MRI together with phenotypic information. This study therefore investigates whether a compact 3D architecture can jointly model structural MRI and phenotypic information while substantially reducing parameter requirements. To this end, we propose \emph{ADHDNet}, a lightweight adaptive multimodal 3D CNN. The main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{lightweight 3D MRI encoder} based on depthwise-separable Conv3D for parameter-efficient volumetric representation learning.
  \item Integration of a single \textbf{3D efficient channel attention (ECA)} module for channel-wise recalibration of the encoder output.
  \item An \textbf{adaptive gated fusion} mechanism for sample-dependent integration of MRI and phenotypic representations.
  \item An \textbf{evaluation of parameter and computational efficiency} against a conventional Conv3D multimodal model on a multisite ADHD-200 cohort, complemented by 5-fold cross-validation and a component-wise ablation study.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
%======================================================================

\subsection{MRI-Based and Multimodal ADHD Classification}
Earlier approaches to MRI-based ADHD classification generally extracted neuroanatomical or functional-connectivity features and applied conventional classifiers~\cite{zhangjames2023,tian2024review,lohani2025review}. Lohani and Rana~\cite{lohani2023} combined gray-matter volume and cortical-thickness features with personal characteristics and achieved 75\% accuracy with repeated ten-fold cross\-validation. More recent studies learn representations directly from neuroimaging data: CNNs have been applied to fMRI~\cite{firouzi2024,gulhan2025}, graph-theoretical features of resting-state networks have been combined with ensemble classifiers~\cite{zamanzadeh2024}, graph convolutional networks with multi-head attention have been applied to hybrid-order brain networks~\cite{wu2025hagcn}, and Pedrollo et al.~\cite{pedrollo2026} reported CNN ensemble-based classification of resting-state fMRI. Phenotypic variables such as age, sex, medication status, and clinical ratings can complement imaging features: Deepika et al.~\cite{deepika2024} combined resting-state functional connectivity with phenotypic data, raising accuracy to 77.59\%. A recent preprint has also explored 3D structural MRI and clinical phenotypes with adaptive gated fusion, reporting 87\% accuracy on the Peking-1 subset of ADHD-200.% TODO: add \cite{...} once the 2026 preprint's bibliographic details are verified; remove this sentence if they cannot be.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Existing studies have shown the value of multimodal information and, separately, of parameter-efficient design, but few combine both by processing volumetric structural MRI together with phenotypic information under an explicit parameter budget. Most also do not report model size or computational cost (Table~\ref{tab:literature}). ADHDNet is designed to address this combination.

\subsection{Dataset}
Experiments used the preprocessed anatomical component of the publicly available ADHD-200 dataset~\cite{adhd200consortium2012,bellec2017}, comprising 575 successfully processed subjects (222 ADHD, 353 controls) from six sites. The Pittsburgh site contained 78 controls but no ADHD subjects; since it does not provide both classes required for binary discrimination, it was excluded. The final cohort comprised 497 subjects (222 ADHD, 275 controls) from five sites, as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:dataset}.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Phenotypic Features}
Each NIfTI volume was resized to $64\times64\times64$ voxels by trilinear interpolation and min-max normalized to $[0,1]$. No additional skull stripping, registration, bias correction, or segmentation was applied, to keep the pipeline compact and reproducible. Five phenotypic variables were used: age, sex, medication status, site, and ADHD Index. Missing values (coded \texttt{-999}) were imputed with the training-set median (continuous) or mode (categorical); age and ADHD Index were z-scored with training-set statistics, and categorical variables were scaled to $[0,1]$.

\section{Proposed ADHDNet}
%======================================================================

ADHDNet consists of a lightweight 3D MRI encoder, a compact phenotypic encoder, and an adaptive gated fusion module (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The complete model contains 71,552 trainable parameters.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/fig1_architecture.png}
\caption{Architecture of ADHDNet: a depthwise-separable 3D MRI encoder with 3D ECA, an MLP phenotypic encoder, and adaptive gated fusion followed by a classification head.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Lightweight 3D MRI Encoder.}
The encoder consists of three blocks with channel progression $1\rightarrow24\rightarrow48\rightarrow96$. Each block applies a depthwise-separable convolution~\cite{chollet2017xception,howard2017mobilenets} (a $3\times3\times3$ depthwise kernel followed by a $1\times1\times1$ pointwise convolution), batch normalization~\cite{ioffe2015bn}, ReLU, and $2\times2\times2$ max pooling, so the resolution falls from $64^3$ to $8^3$. Whereas a standard 3D convolution with kernel size $K$ requires $K^3C_{in}C_{out}$ weights, the depthwise-separable form requires only $K^3C_{in}+C_{in}C_{out}$. The output feature map is recalibrated by 3D ECA, globally average-pooled to 96 dimensions, and projected to a 128-dimensional embedding $z_{MRI}$.

\subsubsection{3D Efficient Channel Attention.}
A single 3D extension of ECA~\cite{wang2020eca} is applied to the $96\times8^3$ output of the third block, computing $F_{att}=A(F)\odot F$. The channel weights $A(F)$ are obtained by global average pooling, a 1D convolution across channels with adaptively chosen kernel size ($k=3$ for 96 channels), and a sigmoid, so the module adds only three parameters.

\subsubsection{Phenotypic Encoder and Adaptive Gated Fusion.}
In parallel, a $5\rightarrow32\rightarrow32$ MLP with batch normalization, ReLU, and dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout} encodes the phenotypic features into $h_P\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$. In the spirit of gated multimodal units~\cite{arevalo2017gmu}, a gating network computes $g=\sigma(W_2\,\delta(W_1[z_{MRI};h_P]))\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$ from the 160-dimensional concatenation, where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid and $\delta$ the ReLU. With $z_P\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$ a linear projection of $h_P$, the fused representation is
\begin{equation}
z_F = g \odot z_{MRI} + (1-g) \odot z_P,
\end{equation}
so the relative contribution of each modality is determined per sample. A $128\rightarrow64\rightarrow2$ classification head with dropout produces the class probabilities.

\section{Experimental Setup}
%======================================================================

\subsubsection{Training.}
Models were implemented in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} and trained with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} (learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batch size 8, at most 150 epochs with early stopping (patience 15), class-weighted cross-entropy with label smoothing~\cite{szegedy2016inception} of 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0, and cosine annealing~\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr}. Training volumes were randomly flipped along each axis with probability 0.5, the checkpoint with the highest validation AUC was retained, and all random seeds were fixed to 42.

\subsubsection{Comparator.}
A conventional Conv3D multimodal model was implemented in the same framework: four standard Conv3D blocks ($32\rightarrow64\rightarrow128\rightarrow256$ channels) with 3D ECA after blocks 2--4, generalized-mean pooling, a 256-dimensional embedding, a $5\rightarrow32\rightarrow64\rightarrow64$ phenotypic MLP, the same gated fusion, and a $256\rightarrow128\rightarrow64\rightarrow2$ head (1,376,080 parameters).

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol.}
(i) \emph{Held-out validation:} a stratified 80:20 split yielded 397 training and 100 validation subjects (45 ADHD, 55 controls). Because the validation set was also used for early stopping and model selection, these results are held-out validation, not independent test, performance. (ii) \emph{Cross-validation:} ADHDNet and the comparator were evaluated with stratified 5-fold cross-validation on all 497 subjects (at most 60 epochs, patience 10), taking fold metrics at the epoch of best validation AUC. (iii) \emph{Ablation:} variants were trained on the same held-out split with the same budget. In all settings, imputation and normalization statistics were fitted on training subjects only. Accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, precision, negative predictive value (NPV), and F1-score were computed with ADHD as the positive class. FLOPs were counted with PyTorch's \texttt{FlopCounterMode} for one $64^3$ volume, and latency was averaged over 50 runs after 10 warm-up passes on a CUDA GPU.% TODO: name the GPU model used for the latency benchmark

\subsection{Held-Out Classification Performance}
On the held-out validation set (Table~\ref{tab:results}), ADHDNet achieved 90.00\% accuracy and an AUC of 0.9543 (best epoch 60; early stopping at epoch 75). It correctly classified 43 of 45 ADHD subjects and 47 of 55 controls (Fig.~\ref{fig:roc_cm}). Of the 10 errors, 8 were false positives and 2 false negatives, so sensitivity (95.56\%) exceeded specificity (85.45\%) and NPV (95.92\%) exceeded precision (84.31\%): on this cohort, a negative prediction was more reliable than a positive one.

\subsection{Cross-Validation and Computational Efficiency}
Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares ADHDNet with the conventional Conv3D comparator. In 5-fold cross-validation, ADHDNet achieved $87.52\pm4.61\%$ accuracy (per-fold range 79.80--90.91\%) and an AUC of $0.9512\pm0.0169$, against $86.51\pm3.64\%$ and $0.9566\pm0.0197$ for the comparator (Fig.~\ref{fig:cv_eff_abl}a). Discrimination was therefore comparable, while ADHDNet had a slightly higher mean accuracy, sensitivity, and F1-score and a less variable sensitivity. The held-out accuracy of 90.00\% lies at the upper end of the cross-validated range, so 87.52\% is the more representative estimate. ADHDNet uses 94.8\% fewer parameters (19.2$\times$) and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs (35.2$\times$), reducing FP32 weight storage from 5.25 to 0.27\,MiB (Fig.~\ref{fig:cv_eff_abl}b). Latency fell by 1.8$\times$ and batched throughput rose by 2.7$\times$; the speed-up is smaller than the FLOP reduction because small 3D models do not saturate the GPU.

The phenotypic branch had by far the largest effect: without it (A2), accuracy fell to 54.00\% and AUC to 0.5644, close to chance, indicating that under the present preprocessing the MRI branch alone provides little discriminative information. Removing ECA (A1) reduced accuracy by 3.0 points and AUC by 0.0056 for a module with three parameters. Concatenation (A3) gave slightly lower accuracy but slightly higher AUC than gating, so this split shows no clear advantage of gated fusion. Standard convolutions (A4) matched the full model with 3.1$\times$ more parameters, confirming that depthwise-separable factorization reduces model size without loss of accuracy. With 100 validation subjects, one subject corresponds to one percentage point, so differences of a few points should be interpreted with caution.

%======================================================================
\section{Discussion}
%======================================================================

ADHDNet jointly models structural MRI and phenotypic information at a small fraction of the cost of a conventional 3D model, reaching comparable cross-validated discrimination with 94.8\% fewer parameters and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs. The ablation attributes this efficiency mainly to depthwise-separable convolution, while the three-parameter ECA module yielded a small accuracy gain and gated fusion performed on par with concatenation.

The most important finding, however, is the dominance of the phenotypic branch: the MRI-only variant performed close to chance, whereas every variant retaining phenotypic input achieved an AUC above 0.94. The phenotypic input includes the ADHD Index, a clinical symptom rating closely related to the diagnostic label, and site, which is correlated with class proportions (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). The reported accuracy should therefore not be read as evidence that structural MRI alone, as represented here, discriminates ADHD, nor that ADHDNet outperforms prior studies, which use different modalities and protocols (Table~\ref{tab:literature}). The contribution is instead a \emph{parameter-efficient multimodal 3D architecture} together with an evaluation that makes the contribution of each component explicit.

\subsubsection{Limitations.}
Performance depends predominantly on phenotypic features, potentially the ADHD Index; held-out, cross-validation, and ablation metrics were taken at the epoch of best validation AUC and no independent external test set was used; cross-site generalization was not evaluated; the ablation used a single split without repeated runs; the simple preprocessing may limit the anatomical information available to the MRI branch; and latency was measured on a single GPU.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%======================================================================

This paper presented ADHDNet, a lightweight multimodal 3D CNN that combines depthwise-separable convolution, efficient channel attention, and adaptive gated fusion of structural MRI and phenotypic information. On 497 ADHD-200 subjects, it achieved 90.00\% held-out accuracy (AUC 0.9543) and $87.52\pm4.61\%$ cross-validated accuracy with 71,552 parameters and 0.19 GFLOPs, 94.8\% fewer parameters and 97.2\% fewer FLOPs than a conventional Conv3D comparator at comparable AUC. The ablation showed that the phenotypic branch supplies most of the discriminative information. Future work will evaluate the model without the ADHD Index and site, strengthen the MRI representation through improved preprocessing, and perform cross-site and external validation.
